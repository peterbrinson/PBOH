Lore and backstory are worldbuilding skills that are likely out of scale of the student.  Tolkien needed backstory because he was building a mythology spanning thousands of years, therefore his books took years to write.  

In games, the instinct to write lore is a distracton from the essential question: **what does the player experience?**

## Lore-dumping

Defined as delivering world backstory, history, or mythology in an undramatized block — exposition, cutscenes, text walls — rather than letting information emerge through experience. Lore-dumping is when the world stops moving so it can explain itself; it conflicts with the aspirations of the [[Catalysts of Speculation]]. 

## The Provocation

Lore is worldbuilding that failed to manifest through situations. If it's worthwhile, put it in the playable experience. 

Concerning projects of enormous scale, Peter Jackson said the hardest part of Lord of the Rings was backstory - avoiding awkward flashbacks.   

## The Better Question

Not: *what is the history of this world?*
But: *what can be communicated from inside of it (briefly)?*

History and depth can be implied through details — a texture, a sound, even a sign in a language you can't read — without manifesting them comprehensively.

## Games That Imply History Without Explaining It
- [[Game - What Remains of Edith Finch|What Remains of Edith Finch]] — generations of family history felt through a single house
- [[Game - Return of the Obra Dinn|Return of the Obra Dinn]] — an entire ship's history reconstructed from physical evidence alone - this is the core play experience
- [[Game - Firewatch|Firewatch]] — the Wyoming wilderness feels ancient and layered through environmental design
