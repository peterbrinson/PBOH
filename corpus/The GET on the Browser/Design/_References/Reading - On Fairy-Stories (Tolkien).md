**Author:** J.R.R. Tolkien · **Year:** 1947 · **Type:** Essay

Tolkien's argument for why fantasy matters — and what it demands of its maker. The essay introduces his concept of **sub-creation**, which is the closest thing worldbuilding has to a founding philosophy.

---

## Sub-Creation

Tolkien argues that humans are *sub-creators* — we make secondary worlds because we are ourselves made. The impulse to build a world isn't escapism; it's an expression of something fundamental about being human.

> *"We make in our measure and in our derivative mode, because we are made: and not only made, but made in the image and likeness of a Maker."*

The primary world (reality) is the raw material. The sub-creator recombines its elements into a **secondary world** — something new that runs on its own internal logic.

This is exactly the distinction [[Mark Wolf|Wolf]] (p.17) draws when he says fictional worlds exist next to the real world, not inside it.

---

## Secondary Belief

A secondary world succeeds when it induces what Tolkien calls **Secondary Belief** — the reader's imaginative absorption. The world has to earn it.

His example: a green sun. You can invent one. But for it to work, the world around it has to be coherent enough that the green sun feels plausible.

This maps directly to [[Mark Wolf|Wolf]]'s concept of **plausibility** — the world earns belief — and to [[Consistency and Internal Logic]], which is what makes secondary belief possible in the first place.
