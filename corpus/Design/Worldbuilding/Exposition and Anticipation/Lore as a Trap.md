*Part of the [[Small Worlds]] framework.*

Lore and backstory are worldbuilding skills that are likely out of scale of the student.  Tolkien needed backstory because he was building a mythology spanning thousands of years, therefore his books took years to write.  Students have less time.  

In games, the instinct to write lore is a way of avoiding the essential question: **what does the player experience?**

## Lore-dumping

Defined as delivering world backstory, history, or mythology in an undramatized block — exposition, cutscenes, text walls — rather than letting information emerge through experience. Lore-dumping is when the world stops moving so it can explain itself; it conflicts with the aspirations of the [[Catalysts of Speculation]]. (An [[Awkward Exposition|in-game codex]] is the graceful exception — opt-in and player-paced, so the world never has to stop.)

## The Provocation

*Lore is worldbuilding that failed to manifest through situations.* If it's worthwhile, it should be in the playable experience. 

Concerning projects of enormous scale, Peter Jackson said the hardest part of Lord of the Rings was backstory - making the flashback not awkward. 

## The Better Question

Not: *what is the history of this world?*
But: *what can be communicated from inside of it (briefly)?*

History and depth can be implied through details — a texture, a sound, even a sign in a language you can't read — without manifesting them comprehensively.

## Games That Imply History Without Explaining It
- [[Game - What Remains of Edith Finch|What Remains of Edith Finch]] — generations of family history felt through a single house
- [[Game - Return of the Obra Dinn|Return of the Obra Dinn]] — an entire ship's history reconstructed from physical evidence alone - this is the core play experience
- [[Game - Firewatch|Firewatch]] — the Wyoming wilderness feels ancient and layered through environmental design
