Mark Wolf (p.43): consistency is the degree to which world details are **plausible, feasible, and without contradiction**.

Rather than strive to make a world realistic (as we don't know what that means), aim to make it *consistent*. The rules can be surprising, even impossible, but once established, breaking them could be trouble.

## The Road Runner - We Learn

The Road Runner cartoon never violates its own physics, however absurd the physics are. Wile E. Coyote survives explosions, falls, and being flattened.  With many of these moments, a new [[Wolf's Taxonomy of Invention|invention]] is introduced.
- 7:10 — painting a tunnel onto a cliff face
- 8:30 — skiing on air
- 14:25 — iconic fall
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_mHcWWOa3aI

![[wile-e-coyote-1.jpg]]

In effect, the outcome of each trap is to teach us one of the world's rules. 
His traps leads to surprises (rather than successes). The surprises (a new rule we're learning) work because the prior rules are established to work as a system. 
Cartoonish gravity, speed, specific materials (bombs, meat grinders, labeled boxes of gadgets) — these form a consistent system.

>A note about narrative vs play: if this were a game, we would try the same worthwhile approaches until we succeed, or at least more than once.  Wile E. Coyote does not retry his ideas.

## Game Examples
- [[Game - Portal|Portal]] — a pinnacle example of how rule consistency and incrementally teaching the player those rules work together.  For example, when the ability to place the second portal (orange), the logic otherwise does not change. 
- [[Game - Baba Is You|Baba Is You]] — rules are literally visible on screen as word blocks. When you rewrite them, the world changes.  
- [[Game - Inscryption|Inscryption]] — the card game's internal rules are consistent even as the meta-layer shifts around them. 
- [[Game - Rain World|Rain World]] — the ecosystem runs on its own schedule regardless of the player's impact.
