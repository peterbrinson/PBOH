Lore and backstory are worldbuilding skills that are likely out of scale of the student.  Tolkien needed backstory because he was building a mythology spanning thousands of years, therefore his books took years to write.  Students have less time.  

In games, the instinct to write lore is a way of avoiding the harder question: **what does the player experience?**

## Lore-dumping

Defined as delivering world backstory, history, or mythology in an undramatized block — exposition, cutscenes, text walls, [[In-Game Codexes|in-game codexes]] — rather than letting information emerge through experience.  Lore-dumping is when the world stops moving so it can explain itself. The opposite of [[Catalysts of Speculation]].

## The Provocation

*Lore is worldbuilding that failed to find a form.* If it's interesting, it should be in the work. 

Concerning projects of enormous scale, Peter Jackson said the hardest part of Lord of the Rings was backstory - making the flashback not awkward. 

## The Better Question

Not: *what is the history of this world?*
But: *what does this world feel like to be inside of it (briefly)?*

History and depth can be implied through details — a texture, a sound, a sign in a language you can't read — without manifesting them comprehensively.

## Games That Imply History Without Explaining It
- [[Game - What Remains of Edith Finch|What Remains of Edith Finch]] — generations of family history felt through a single house
- [[Game - Return of the Obra Dinn|Return of the Obra Dinn]] — an entire ship's history reconstructed from physical evidence alone
- [[Game - Firewatch|Firewatch]] — the Wyoming wilderness feels ancient and layered through environmental design
