## Summary

A body of short tales, each a few lines long, traditionally credited to Aesop — a storyteller of ancient Greece — though most were gathered by others across the centuries after him. Each fable is a self-contained little story, usually starring animals who talk and act like people, and each ends by making a single point: a moral. They began as spoken stories and were only written down and collected generations later, which is part of why so many unattributed tales ended up under Aesop's name.

The best-known example is **[The Boy Who Cried Wolf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Boy_Who_Cried_Wolf).** A shepherd boy, bored on the hillside, twice tricks the villagers by shouting that a wolf is attacking his flock; they come running, find nothing, and go back annoyed. When a real wolf finally comes and the boy cries out in earnest, no one believes him — and the flock is lost. The moral: a liar is not believed even when he tells the truth.

## Relevance to Worldbuilding

Each fable is a compact model of a [[Bounded Worlds|Finite]] world — a world complete in a handful of lines, with nothing outside what the story needs. **The Boy Who Cried Wolf** is the clean example: the world is exactly the pasture, the boy, the sheep, the villagers, and the wolf. There is no wider town, no history, no elsewhere. 
Also useful across the [[Situated Player Roles]] as a study in economy: how little a world can contain and still say something.
