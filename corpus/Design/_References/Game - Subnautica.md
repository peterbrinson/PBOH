## What It Is

The reference 3D survival game. Your ship crashes on an alien ocean planet and you're alone underwater. You gather resources, craft tools, build bases, and manage oxygen, hunger, and thirst while descending into ever-deeper, darker biomes — and slowly uncover what happened to the planet's vanished inhabitants and why no rescue is coming.

## Survival *as* Worldbuilding

Most survival games are systems first and world second. Subnautica is the opposite — its survival loop is inseparable from its world:

- **The world's nature sets the rules.** Oxygen, pressure, and light aren't UI numbers bolted on; they're the [[Nature and Invented Physics|invented nature]] of an ocean planet, and they dictate how you can move and how deep you dare go. The biome *is* the difficulty curve.
- **Descent is the dread.** There are almost no weapons. The threat is the world itself — the dark, the depth, the things larger than you that you can only flee. Fear comes from going down, not from combat.
- **The story is in the water.** What happened here — a crashed ship, an alien plague, a long-dead Precursor civilization — is reconstructed from wreckage, data logs, and ruins. Pure [[Catalysts of Speculation|environmental storytelling]]: the world existed before you and withholds most of its answers.

That makes it a strong **Investigator** (read the wreck for what happened) and **Traveler** (the ocean is to be moved through and witnessed) example — survival mechanics carrying a genuine world, not just a stat economy.

## What to Pull From It For a Survival Project

Before reaching for crafting menus and hunger bars, ask what Subnautica asks: what does *this* world's nature force the player to do, and what does the place withhold that pulls them deeper?
