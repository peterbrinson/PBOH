# Week 5.1 — Quandaries

*Goals, rewards, and punishments — why we do the things we do, and how games turn a choice into a dilemma.*

This session opens with peer playtests of the Movement Project, then turns to decision-making: why players chase rewards (even at the cost of the experience itself), how games present paths with known and unknown trade-offs, and the biases — like the sunk cost fallacy — that pull us toward non-optimal choices. From there it surveys the major *kinds* of choice games offer, building toward the Quandary Project: a level designed around a single difficult decision.

## Goals and Rewards

Why do we do the things we do? Let's start with a confession about decision-making.

When I go somewhere on vacation, I look for a touristy shop where I can buy a Christmas ornament and a sticker I can put on my luggage. I *could* be spending that time experiencing the place I've traveled to — but instead I'm drawn to collect a memento of the trip.

![[week5.1-joshua-tree-ornament.png]]
*A Joshua Tree ornament — the kind of memento that pulls attention away from the experience itself.*

![[week5.1-joshua-tree-sticker.png]]
*And the matching luggage sticker. The reward of *having collected* can outweigh the reward of *being there*.*

Games run on the same impulse. Much of play is players pursuing goals and rewards — and a designer's job is to understand what makes a reward worth chasing, and what it costs the player to chase it.

## Paths — Known and Unknown

Sometimes a game offers multiple paths with known trade-offs. One path might be riskier but offer greater rewards; another might take more time but offer a greater chance of success.

Other times a game offers a choice between a *known* path and an *unknown* one. How do players make that decision? Recall the chapter of *Algorithms to Live By* about exploration and exploitation.

![[week5.1-inscryption.jpg]]
*Inscryption (2021) — a branching map of nodes, where each step is a choice between known and unknown rewards.*

![[week5.1-death-stranding.png]]
*Death Stranding (2019) — route planning across terrain, weighing the safe path against the fast or rewarding one.*

## The Sunk Cost Fallacy

Human beings have natural biases that cause us to prefer non-optimal choices. One of these is the **sunk cost fallacy** — our tendency to want to continue along a path we've already invested in, even when it would be objectively better to abandon that investment and start fresh.

## Builds

Something you choose at the *beginning* of the game that affects your experience throughout. In *Mass Effect*, you choose a class at the start that shapes your abilities and weapons for the rest of the game. In *Hades*, you choose a weapon that determines your attacks for that run. In *Magic: The Gathering*, you build a deck that determines which cards you have access to and how likely you are to draw them.

![[week5.1-hades.jpg]]
*Hades (2020).*

## Progression

Sometimes the game presents choices *as you play* that shape your strategy and let you determine your play style. In *Baldur's Gate 3*, you choose your class and class options every time you level up. In *Hades*, you choose whether to seek boons from a particular god, and which one. In *Deus Ex*, you level up through a tech tree that lets you focus on combat effectiveness or stealth.

![[week5.1-baldurs-gate-3.png]]
*Baldur's Gate 3 (2023).*

## Emotional Weight

A game's dramatic elements can make choices difficult — or even present a situation as a dilemma when there isn't really a meaningful choice to make. Game narratives present choices all the time, with varying stakes. Games with units or parties can create dilemmas by putting characters in danger with trade-offs.

Consider the Companion Cube from *Portal*, created opportunistically to manufacture a moment of dilemma for the player.

![[week5.1-portal-companion-cube.jpg]]
*Portal (2007).*

## Prisoner's Dilemma

Games can use social pressure and game theory to create difficult choices, especially when other players are involved. *Dead Last* is a tabletop game in which two players split a pot of loot: each can choose a small share or a large share, but if *both* choose a large share, neither gets anything — and the loot is distributed among the other players instead.

![[week5.1-zero-escape.jpg]]
*Zero Escape: Virtue's Last Reward (2012).*
