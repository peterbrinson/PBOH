## The Four Arcs of Experience

Let's recall the four arcs of experience presented this semester.

**Narrative.** Stories are about characters who act and change. Characters face difficult choices that spur them to action and force them to reflect. Each choice is an opportunity to surprise the audience, and pacing is made of small and large surprises strung together. Sometimes the dilemma is more interesting than the outcome — we want to wonder what they were thinking.

**Mechanics.** Mechanics define how the player relates to the player character and the game world. Changing the mechanics causes the player to reevaluate their experience. Mechanics that reflect the character's experience of the world draw the player into the character's story; changing them to reflect the character's choices and growth makes the player invested in the character's dilemmas.

**Subtext and Theme.** Games often follow a surrealist pattern of presenting illogic and prompting the player to make meaning out of it. Stories use metaphors to explore complex ideas in novel ways; subtext suggests there is meaning to be found if we work to unlock it, and controlling metaphors serve as the keys. Surrealism asks us to reconcile the conscious with the unconscious, turning it into metaphor and subtext.

**Agency.** Games let players feel self-efficacy — the belief that their actions make a difference. Agency is subjective: usually we care more about how much the player *seems* to have than how much they actually do. Self-conscious emotions like guilt, pride, fiero, naches, and embarrassment are based on the player reflecting on their own actions. Games can employ "narrative listening" and use feedback to increase the player's sense of agency.

## Book Club — VS

A head-to-head comparison exercise. For each prompt, weigh the two games on the table against each other:

- Which game is most fun?
- Which game speaks to the underdog in us all?
- Which is best enjoyed with others?
- Most style, least substance?
- Most substance, least style?
- Best dramatic premise?
- Best designed objective?
- Tells the best story?

## In-Class Game Analysis

Work through these games, each paired with an analytical lens. Rotate the lenses across the list — formal/dramatic/dynamic elements, the narrative/mechanical/metaphorical arcs, agency, and the emotions evoked.

A few of the games on the table:

- **La Madriguera** (2021) — *What are the formal elements? The rules, procedures, objectives, conflicts?*
- **Thirty Flights of Loving** (2012) — *What are the dramatic elements? Premise, characters, visual style?*
- **Before Your Eyes** (2020) — *What are the dynamic elements? How do affordances and constraints create an experience through play?*


- **Dr. Langeskov, The Tiger, and the Terribly Cursed Emerald** (2015) — *What is the narrative arc? Who is the protagonist and what do they want?*
- **Spaceplan** (2017) — *What is the mechanical arc? What changes and what stays the same?*
- **Gorogoa** (2017) — *What is the metaphorical arc? What subtext can be extracted from a close reading of its symbols?*


- **Frog Fractions** (2012) — *What agency does the game present? What self-reflective emotions does it play with?*
- **Umurangi Generation** (2020) — *What are the formal elements?*
- **The Roottrees are Dead** (2023) — *What are the dramatic elements?*
- **Her Story** (2015) — *What are the dynamic elements?*


- **Reigns** (2016) — *What is the narrative arc, and how is it presented to the audience?*
- **Free Will** (2024) — *What is the mechanical arc?*
- **We Become What We Behold** (2016) — *What is the metaphorical arc?*
- **Papers, Please** (2014) — *What agency does the game present?*


- **Gone Home** (2013), **Meteorfall** (2020), and others — *What are the dominant emotions evoked, and what techniques are used to evoke them?*

## Two Lenses

Elements of game design (formal, dramatic, dynamic) are one way to construct and deconstruct games. Arcs of experience (narrative, mechanical, metaphorical, self-reflective) are another. **How are you using these tools in constructing your prototypes?**

## Reading — Engines of Experience

*Designing Games* by Tynan Sylvester, Chapter 1: *Engines of Experience*. Read by next Thursday. This chapter suggests another model for thinking about games — focused on evoking an emotional response in players — and presents an extensive list of emotional triggers you can use to create designed experiences.
