## My History as a Game Developer

I did my undergraduate in German Literature, but I'd taken a few CS classes and taught myself some (rudimentary, at the time) web technologies. After graduating I worked as a technical writer and then as various kinds of web developer. While earning my MFA at Parsons, I built projects in Processing and Flash+ActionScript 3, played with brand-new web APIs like the accelerometer and real-time communication, and made my thesis project in Unity 3.3. Then came a small New York game company (Flash+AS3 for an iOS shooter), a stint as UI/UX designer and lead programmer on an iOS app that mimicked Adobe Illustrator (ReactJS front end, Node.js build, a cloud back end calculating building energy impacts), and an interactive data visualization in d3.js.


Then I came to USC. In the twelve years since, alongside primarily using Unity (plus an iPad LiDAR AR app), I've also made a simple Alexa app, extensively customized Twine, written an Inform game, made simple Arduino toys, worked with Unreal and Virtual Humans, and built a React/Java webapp for the IndieCade jury system. I spent a summer on an R&D team prototyping math-learning games for toddlers (Unity on iPads), and built a data-visualization tool using Neo4j (a graph database) and Cytoscape.


Along the way: *Walden, a game* (2010, originally in the licensed Gamebryo Lightspeed engine, later rewritten in Unity); my MFA thesis *Grayline* (2011, a co-op detective game for iPad in Flash — which stopped being buildable when Apple changed iOS provisioning); *FutureBound* (2013, Unity, about navigating middle school); and a run of experimental collaborations with Martzi Campos and Jesse Vigil.

## Technology Changes

That list is the whole point: **technology changes, and learning to work with new tools and processes is a critical skill for a game developer.** Many studios use Unreal and Unity — but the truth is most use proprietary engines, and even more use plugins, tools, and custom mods you've never seen. Having experience with a studio's specific technology is great, but it's far more important to be *willing and able to learn new things*.
